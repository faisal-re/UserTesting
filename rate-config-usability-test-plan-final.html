Rate configuration: usability test plan

Testing whether users can find the merged rate page, work their way through a rate update, read the terms on screen, and see what a change will do before they confirm it.

1. OVERVIEW

Why this study exists

Grounded in the Fuel and Surcharge Management BRD. This section still needs a pass against the document itself.

The design merges fuel, surcharges, kilometre rates and minimum rate into a single Rate Configuration page and moves the whole thing into Finance. Rate settings used to live inside Enterprise → Connections, reached by opening a specific connection. Both the merge and the move are new, and neither has been put in front of a user.

The study covers finding that page, working through an update, the vocabulary on the page, and the confirm step that ends every edit.

Objectives and coverage

Four objectives, five blocks.

IDObjective
O1Whether users find rate configuration when told only that the rate types now share one page
O2Whether users can work out the path for updating a rate and for creating a new rule
O3Whether the rate type names and the Default and Special pair mean anything to the people using them
O4Whether the confirm step tells users what is about to change, and what they expect it to show

What we expect to see

Written down beforehand so a result cannot be reinterpreted afterwards.

  • Told the rate types are merged onto one page, users still look under Enterprise or Connections rather than Finance.
  • Users can update an existing rate but do not distinguish that from creating a new rule.
  • Fuel rate and surcharge get conflated, and "article rate" is not read as a kilometre rate.
  • "Special" reads as urgent, temporary or promotional rather than as an override.
  • Users can describe the value change from the confirm step, but do not mention that booked orders are repriced retroactively.

Participants

Whon
Planners, ops or finance5

Screener. Have you set or changed a rate in the last three months?

2. PHASE 1

Phase 1: Lyssna study

Five blocks, about twelve minutes, run in this order.

Block 1

Where would you go?

Single use

Purpose. Participants are told only that fuel and the other rate types now live together on one page. Nothing else. What we want to see is where that description sends them: whether a merged rate page reads as a Finance thing, or as something that still belongs inside a connection. It is a case study in finding the entry point, and it works exactly once, because after this block every participant knows where the page lives.

Brief given
"Fuel rate, surcharges, kilometre rates and minimum rate have been merged into one page."
Stimulus
Static dashboard and navigation. No rate screens visible.
Prompt
"A subcontractor's fuel rate went up this week. Where would you click first?"
Format
First click on an image, followed by one open text field: why there?
Look at
Where the clicks land, where the misses cluster, and the reasoning each person gives

Reading the resultWhere the misses cluster matters more than how many there are. Everyone landing on Enterprise says the old mental model is intact and the move has to be signposted from there. A scatter across the nav says the brief gave people nothing to reason from, and the page name itself is doing no work.

Block 2

Work the path

Observational

Purpose. This is about whether the whole path makes sense, not whether a task gets ticked off. Two things are being watched: whether people can work out how to update an existing rate, and whether they can tell that apart from creating a new rule. The design treats those as the same button, and it is not obvious they should be. Beyond that, this is the one block where participants are simply moving through the prototype and saying what they notice, so it is also where unexpected reactions surface.

Stimulus
Clickable prototype, opened at the group list
Prompt
Two goals, given in sequence. "Update the fuel rate for this subcontractor." Then: "Set up a different rate for one specific carrier."
Format
Prototype task with think aloud recording
Look at
  • The path taken, and where it diverges from the intended one
  • Whether updating and creating are treated as different actions
  • Hesitation, backtracking, and what participants say aloud
Not recorded
Completion, time on task

Reading the resultThe output is a written account of the routes people took and what they said, with the recordings attached. Two or more people stalling at the same step is worth acting on. One person taking an odd route is worth a question in the interview. No clock, because a clock pushes people past the moments worth hearing about.

Block 3

Rate type terminology

Three parts

Purpose. The merged page asks people to pick between four rate types, and three of the four tabs label their value column identically as Article Rate. This block goes in two directions: first what the existing words mean to people unprompted, then what they would call the same thing themselves. The second half matters because a failed label is only half a finding. Knowing what people call it is what makes the rewrite possible rather than another guess.

Part 1
Open text. "In your own words, what does each of these set?" for fuel rate, surcharge, kilometre rate, minimum rate and article rate. No options offered, so nothing supplies the answer.
Part 2
Given a plain description of each thing the system actually does, choose the label that fits best from a short list.
Part 3
Open text. If none of the options fit, what would you call it?
Look at
Which terms come back described distinctly, which pairs get conflated, which labels win in part 2, and any word proposed by more than one person in part 3

Reading the resultPart 1 shows whether the current words work. Parts 2 and 3 supply the replacement. A conflation between fuel rate and surcharge is a copy problem. Article rate is different: a fuel per kilometre value exists both as a fuel range and as a catalogue article, so confusion there points at the data model and belongs with product rather than in a copy pass.

Block 4

Default Rate against Special Rate

Purpose. These two words carry the entire override model: a narrower rule beating a broader one. The naming comes from the requirements rather than from design, so a poor result here does not licence a rename. It tells us how much supporting copy the tab needs to carry. That is still worth knowing, because someone can complete a task perfectly while misunderstanding what they just did, and watching them succeed would never reveal it.

Prompt
"This screen has two tabs, Default Rate and Special Rate. When would you use each?"
Format
Open text
Look at
Whether Special is described as an override, and where the wrong readings land: urgent, temporary, a discount, a one off

Reading the resultBecause the labels are fixed, the response is a subtitle, tooltip or short description under the tab rather than a new name. The specific wrong reading is what shapes that copy, so record the words people use rather than just marking an answer wrong.

Block 5

The confirm step

Read, then expect

Purpose. The confirm step is the last thing standing between an operator and a change that reprices real invoices. It now carries a lot: a current and new comparison of value, rate type and effective period, a count of affected orders, a line stating that orders already booked in the range will use the new value retroactively, a per order recalculation table, and a third step asking for a reason. So the question is not whether the information is there. It is whether any of it registers in the few seconds someone spends on this screen before clicking through, and in particular whether the retroactive consequence is noticed by anyone who was not looking for it.

Part 1
Show the Confirm Changes step. "You are about to confirm. What exactly is changing, and what happens to work already booked?" Open text.
Part 2
"Is there anything here you would want to check before clicking through?" Open text. Tests whether the retroactive line and the recalculation table are read or skipped.
Part 3
"What would you want a confirmation to tell you before you commit?" Open text, then a multi select to rank what they did not mention: last updated · who changed it last · the reason for the change · the change history · the number of affected orders · the validity period.
Look at
Whether value, rate type and effective period are all named in part 1; whether retroactivity comes up unprompted; what people say about the Reason for Correction step

Reading the resultKeep the open question ahead of the option list, so the menu does not put words in anyone's mouth. Two things are worth watching closely. The NEW column reads as a row of dashes until a value is entered, so if anyone takes that to mean nothing is changing, the problem is the display rather than their understanding. And retroactivity is stated in a single sentence under the order count: if nobody mentions it, a policy with real financial consequences is being communicated in a line people skim past.

3. PHASE 2

Phase 2: follow up interviews

Thirty minutes each, with three of the five. Pull the recordings first.

Three kinds of thing land here. Depth on whatever Phase 1 surfaced, since an unmoderated study shows a result but not the thinking behind it. Questions that only work with a moderator watching, where a yes is too easy to give. And behaviour the design has not defined yet, which cannot be tested unmoderated because participants would be guessing at rules that do not exist.

  1. Warm up, 3 min. Walk me through the last time you changed a rate. Establishes their real workflow before the prototype colours it.
  2. Replay the first click, 6 min. "You went here first. Why there? Where did you expect to end up?" Recovers the mental model behind a single click, which Block 1 records but cannot explain.
  3. Replay the path, 7 min. Play back their Block 2 run and stop at each hesitation. Ask what they were looking for. This is also where to ask whether updating a rate and creating a new rule felt like the same thing or two different things.
  4. The stale rate, 5 min. Show a list where one relation's rate expired weeks ago, with no hint. "Anything here need attention?" Then: how would you expect to find out about this in your normal week? The cue they name is the finding: the validity date, the last updated stamp, or neither.
  5. Open behaviour, 7 min. Three scenarios where the design has no defined answer yet:
    • "The screen says orders already booked in this range will use the new value. Is that what you would expect, and who needs to know?"
    • "You change it at enterprise level. Who else just changed?"
    • "You set a rate for 15 to 20 August, and a rate already covers the whole of August. What should the system do?"
  6. Close, 2 min. If this replaced what you use today, what would you miss? What would you not trust it to get right?
